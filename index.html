A few ideas worth keeping.

01 / CONNECTIONS

One net, one box.

A net groups terminals that must connect. Its half-perimeter wirelength is the width plus the height of their smallest axis-aligned bounding box.

HPWL(net) = (max x − min x)
+ (max y − min y)

Add the estimates for every net. A terminal can move inside its box without changing that net’s HPWL.

02 / SPACE

Count only the extras.

A bin is a region of the placement area. In this point model, capacity limits the number of points in a bin, including the fixed terminals.

overflow = Σ max(0, count − capacity)

Empty space in one bin does not cancel an excess in another. A point on x = 4 belongs to the right bin; on y = 4, to the upper bin. The colours show occupancy, not temperature or measured routing congestion.

03 / THE LOOP

Pull. Spread. Reconnect.

The course reference uses 6 force updates and 3 averaging updates, then 12 rounds of density spreading with a light reconnect step after each round.

Pull→Spread→Reconnect

This is a small educational heuristic. It does not implement full industrial analytical placement, legalization or detailed routing.

04 / EVIDENCE

Keep the conditions.

The designed capacity-2 comparison changes 48.5 / 3 → 52.0 / 0 for HPWL / overflow. The capacity-1 reference changes 52.0 / 2 → 48.1 / 2.

6 points − 4 available places
= at least 2 extra points

Do not compare different anchors as if only density had changed. Report the netlist, fixed terminals, capacity and parameters with the two scores.

Explore further · why 48 is a wirelength lower bound here

With A = (0, 0) and D = (8, 8), the nets A–B and B–D together cost at least 16 Manhattan units. The same is true for A–C and C–D. The A–B–C–D net contains both anchors, so its box costs at least 16. E–F contributes a non-negative amount. The total is therefore at least 48 under these fixed-anchor conditions.

This is a lower bound on the point model’s HPWL. It does not prove that a particular run finds a physically legal or globally optimal chip placement.
